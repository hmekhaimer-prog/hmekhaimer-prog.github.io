Like a Robot Learning to Be Human

A reflection on friendship, trust, and the connections we never learned to protect

  September 2026   12 min read

I spent weeks on this one, overthinking every angle, connecting dots that refused to connect, getting stuck, starting over, getting stuck again. There were moments of paralysis and moments of genuine doubt. I felt illiterate in the topic, not because I lacked information, but because friendship is not something you can think your way through. It has to pass through you first. I did not want to confine myself. I wanted to free my mind, because that is what brings clarity.

Everything I reflected on in The Art of Two about being seen, about emotional language, about the courage to stay present when withdrawal would be easier, applies here with equal force. Listening is not a romantic skill. Empathy is not a couples-therapy concept. A relationship is a relationship. The tools that save one can save the other. While writing this, I felt like a robot learning how to be human.

01The Relationship With No Safety Net

Friendship is the only important relationship that is expected to sustain itself without clear guidelines. Romantic relationships have movies, therapy, dating apps, and entire industries built for their survival. Family has blood, shared history, and obligation. Friendship gets nothing.

In marriage, people exchange vows, plan weddings, invest fortunes. Families share the same house, the same upbringing, the same formative conditions. Friendship has none of that. No vows. No blood. It is held together by nothing but trust. And yet, people reveal things to their friends that they would never reveal to their siblings or their spouses. No obligations, no formalities, no guarantees. Only trust. That is what makes it fragile. That is also what makes it the most authentic connection a person could ever have.

Not everyone understands this instinctively. But some people do. I have one friend who has an extraordinary sense of faith. Once he commits to something, he commits completely: to friends, to family, to himself, to the belief that good things are worth pursuing without proof. He always used to say: humans get recovered by their friends. For the longest time, I treated that as a nice sentiment. Only recently did I begin to understand it as a literal truth.

Loneliness is as toxic for the body as smoking 15 cigarettes a day. Social connection matters more for longevity than diet or exercise. When you have your tribe, your body and mind settle into a kind of safety that no amount of professional success can replicate. Friendship is not a luxury. It is a health requirement. And most of us are quietly failing at it.

Why do we invest the least in the relationship where we feel the most free?

I think the answer begins with how these relationships were formed in the first place.

• • •

02How We Built Friendships Was Always an Accident

Nearly every friendship you have made since childhood was built on circumstantial proximity. Same school, same neighborhood, same club, same university. A group of humans placed at the same location for a period of time, meeting consistently, sharing the same pressures. Friendship was something happening to us, not something we were choosing.

Sociologists have identified two conditions that allow friendship to form organically:

1
Continuous Unplanned Interaction
Seeing someone regularly without scheduling it.
2
Shared Vulnerability
Both people revealing something real.

In school and university, both conditions exist naturally. You share a schedule, a classroom, the weight of exams. You do not need to arrange anything. As we grow older, those conditions vanish. Everyone develops their own rhythm, their own obligations, their own direction.

Some people attempt to build friendships at work, but work only offers the first condition. It blocks the second. Professionalism prevents real sharing. You interact with one dimension of each other repeatedly, and the result is a connection that feels familiar but rarely becomes deep.

Most of what we called friendship was never built on choice. It was built on convenience. When the convenience disappeared, so did the friendship. We just did not have the language to call it loss.

• • •

03Why We Don't Call It Loss

When a close friend slowly drifts away, there is no language for it. No closure. No ceremony. Just silence. And because we never built the habit of maintaining these relationships deliberately, we assume the fading is natural. The fact that it is quiet does not make it small.

Friendships never ran by themselves. They ran because circumstances ran them. Once those circumstances changed, the truth surfaced: these connections needed active care, and most of us had never been taught how to provide it.

In friendship, expectations are rarely spoken. You are always trying to figure it out. When things go wrong, most people choose silence over the discomfort of a conversation that has no script. They would rather let the friendship dissolve than risk the awkwardness of honesty. But the science reveals something counterintuitive: open, empathic discussions about what is wrong tend to produce more intimacy, not less. The tension is already present. The only question is whether you name it or let it decay.

Friendship requires the same investment as any relationship worth keeping: effort, energy, presence, and honest conversation. Treating it as the cherry on top is precisely what kills it. And what holds it together, when there are no vows and no blood, is trust.

• • •

04Trust Is the Religion

Trust is the religion of friendship. It is what keeps people returning to something they cannot see, cannot prove, and cannot guarantee. Like any act of faith, it requires consistency and practice, not just belief.

Brené Brown offers one of the most precise definitions I have encountered: "Trust is making something important to you vulnerable to the actions of someone else. And distrust is the realization that what you shared, what mattered to you, is not safe with them."

She deconstructs trust into seven components, which she calls BRAVING:

B
Boundaries
You respect mine. I respect yours.
R
Reliability
You do what you said. Over and over.
A
Accountability
Mistakes owned. Amends made.
V
Vault
What you share stays with me.
I
Integrity
Values practiced, not only professed.
N
Non-judgment
Fall apart without being judged.
G
Generosity
The most generous interpretation first.

One insight that stopped me: "I don't trust people who don't love themselves and then say I love you." If someone has not learned to trust themselves, their ability to hold trust for someone else remains fragile. Self-love is not vanity. It is the prerequisite of trustworthiness.

Trust is the structure, but what is the meaning of a structure with nothing to fill it? A framework of reliability and boundaries means little if no one is willing to step inside it and be seen. The substance that transforms trust from a concept into a living bond is vulnerability.

• • •

05Vulnerability Is the Currency

We avoid vulnerability because of what it might cost us: judgment, exploitation, the perception of weakness. But the research consistently reveals the opposite: the more people share vulnerably about themselves, the more liked they become, and the more connected others feel to them. The thing you are most afraid to say is often the thing that brings people closest.

For men, this becomes especially complicated. Much of what appears as a friendship problem among men is actually a vulnerability problem in disguise. Men have been socialized to require a "third object" in their friendships: the game on television, the sport they play together, the project they collaborate on. The third object offers something to focus on, a way to be in proximity without ever having to be emotionally direct. It protects against discomfort, but it also prevents depth.

This raises a question that rarely gets asked: why can men sit beside each other for years and never say anything real? Why is it easier to watch a game in silence than to say "I have been struggling"? The answer has a name: homohysteria, the fear among men of being perceived as gay. This fear quietly kills male intimacy. It prevents men from initiating, from expressing affection, from showing genuine emotional interest in another man. Not sexual interest. Human interest. The result is a generation of men surrounded by people but starving for connection.

Vulnerability is not a one-time act. It is a practice. It strengthens with repetition and atrophies with avoidance. And like any practice, it becomes easier the more you realize that the risk of being seen is almost always smaller than the cost of remaining hidden.

But even when trust is present and vulnerability is practiced, friendships still face their most difficult test: what happens when life changes, and the people inside the friendship change with it.

• • •

06The Stretch and The Strain

Friendships are not static. They are living structures that must adapt or quietly collapse. Like muscles, they need stretching to remain flexible. A stretch is healthy: your friend moves cities, enters a marriage, shifts careers, or grows in a direction you did not anticipate. It demands effort, but it strengthens the bond. A strain is what happens when the stretching continues without communication, when the silence accumulates and the distance stops being geographic and becomes emotional. Most friendships do not end in a fight. They end in a slow, unnamed withdrawal.

→
Stretch
Effort Communication Adaptation Bond strengthens
⇥
Strain
Silence Assumptions Withdrawal Bond erodes

There is a concept called Shine Theory: "I don't shine if you don't shine." Instead of competing with your friend's success, you invest in it. You share resources. You invest in their wins as if they were your own.

The best friendships practice identity affirmation: they support who you are becoming, not who they believe you should remain. A friend who projects their own fears onto your decisions is not protecting you. They are protecting their comfort. The friendships that endure are the ones where both people are free to change.

Only 30% of close friendships survive beyond seven years. Not because people are disloyal, but because lives diverge.

Earlier this year, I was visiting the UAE and staying with a friend in Abu Dhabi. Another close friend lived in Dubai. I did not know at the time how far the drive would be, but I decided to go. I had things on my mind. Career questions, personal crossroads, the kind of thoughts that do not sharpen in isolation. I needed his perspective, not because he had answers, but because he had the kind of honesty that makes your own thinking clearer.

I arrived around noon, planning to stay for two hours. I left at nine in the evening. What was supposed to be a quick visit turned into an entire day. We had lunch with his family, went out for a bit, sat for coffee, tested fragrances he had brought from Saudi Arabia, and talked about everything that actually mattered: career direction, politics at work, personal development, and the questions I was genuinely wrestling with. He freed his entire day. Not because I asked him to, but because he understood what it meant that I was there.

As I was leaving, he said something that has stayed with me: "Bro, you drove all the way from Abu Dhabi just to see me. This means a lot." I am not sure I fully understood the weight of what he said in that moment. But looking back, I think what he recognized was not the distance. It was the intention. Whether that single act is enough to sustain a friendship across years and changing circumstances, I honestly do not know. But I think it mattered. And sometimes, that is all you have to go on.

• • •

07Growth Costs Connections

Growth-oriented people often experience friendship as loss. Every level up costs you some connections. Not because you outgrow people, but because the version of you that fit comfortably beside them no longer exists. New priorities form. New values emerge. The overlap shrinks. And the people who once understood you completely begin to feel distant, not because they changed, but because you did.

Some friendships were built for a season. Some were built for a lesson. And some, a rare few, were built to last. The ones that last are not the ones where nothing changed. They are the ones where both people changed and chose to stay anyway.

Lesson
It ended. The growth did not.
Season
Valuable while it lasted.
Lifetime
Both changed. Both stayed.

The mistake is thinking the solution is to stop growing or to stop trying. The solution is to go where your future self already lives.

Friendship, I have come to believe, is not confined to the people outside your bloodline. The feeling of friendship can live inside any relationship: in a sibling who sees you without pretense, in a parent who respects your independence, in a partner who challenges you without controlling you, in a stranger you meet once and remember for years. Friendship is not a category of person. It is a quality of connection. Those who recognize it wherever it appears, and protect it when they find it, are living something most people only talk about.

I do not have the answers. I am not even able to be the kind of friend I have described in this reflection. Not yet. But I believe that naming something is the first step toward understanding it, and understanding is the first step toward doing it differently.
